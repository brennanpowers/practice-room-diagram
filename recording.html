Recording Practice

Zoom H4essential sidecar · 4 tracks · 32-bit float
← Layout diagram Sources & references EQ guide

The recording rig is a sidecar: it travels, and the practice room works exactly as documented without it. Nothing is left behind, nothing in the room chain depends on it. Setup is one recorder, two cables, and under a minute. This page is the operating manual — what to do, what to expect, and what is honestly compromised.

If you remember one thing

Point the recorder at MAIN L. That single act sets the geometry. It puts the lead-vocal wedge roughly 145° off-axis so it doesn't flood the room track, and it keeps MIC 1's singer almost directly behind the mic where the capsules reject them hardest. Aimed the other way — north, toward the band — the lead vocal pours into the room track and the isolation the whole rig exists for stops working.

01 · What you get

Four tracks, and one of them is a free mix

The recorder writes discrete files per source plus an automatic stereo mix, all in 32-bit float, to microSD and over USB simultaneously. The card copy means a USB dropout can't cost you the take.

FileSourceWhat it carries
TrMicBuilt-in X/YThe room from the desk — drums forward, amps, ambience
Tr1Input 1 ← M200 STEREO OUTAll four vocals, on their own fader
Tr2Input 2 ← B-5 on a boomThe room from (500, 350) — even instrument balance
TrLRAutoStereo mix of all three. Listen back without opening a DAW

Four channels across four files — the stereo X/Y counts as two. In a DAW that's three regions (one stereo, two mono) plus the optional mix.

No per-singer control. All four vocals arrive pre-summed from the mixer, so you can move the vocals as a group but not one singer against another. That's the deliberate trade for a setup with no splitters at all — nothing at the microphones changes, and MIC 1 keeps the two-way split it already has. §11 covers what the splitter-based alternatives buy and why they're probably not worth it here.

02 · Setup, in order

Five minutes, and the room is untouched when you leave

Leave these in the room permanently — a mic stand is furniture, not recording gear, and cables are cheaper than friction:

  • A boom stand at (500, 350), set to about 6 ft
  • An XLR run from that boom, coiled at the desk
  • A 3.5 mm → ¼" TS cable from the M200's STEREO OUT, coiled at the desk

You then carry only the recorder and the B-5. Each session:

  1. Recorder on the standing desk at roughly (600, 900), about 4 ft up. Put it on a small stand or foam pad rather than flat on the desk — a hard surface directly under the capsules causes comb filtering from the reflection arriving a fraction of a millisecond behind the direct sound. Keep it off the back edge and away from the wall for the same reason.
  2. Aim the capsules at MAIN L. Screen faces back toward you, roughly northeast. Level, or tilted very slightly up.
  3. Clip the B-5 on the boom (omni capsule) and plug it into Input 2.
  4. Plug the M200 cable into Input 1.
  5. Arm all three channels — the MIC, 1 and 2 buttons. Red light on means armed.
  6. Check the settings below, hit record, and forget about it.

Teardown: unplug two cables, pocket the recorder and the mic. Nothing at the microphones was ever touched, so there is nothing to put back.

Running a splitter-based configuration instead? Steps 3–4 become: insert the splitter at MIC 1 (one leg continues to the M200, the other to an input) and the same at MIC 2 if you're using both. Teardown then means pulling the splitters and restoring the direct mic cables. See §11.

03 · Recorder settings

Set once, then stop thinking about levels

SettingValueWhy
Phantom (In 1)OFFThe M200 feed is line level. Never send phantom down it
Phantom (In 2)ONThe B-5 is a condenser and needs 48 V. Phantom is per-input here, so In 1 stays clean
Lo Cut (In 2)ONKeeps bass-amp and floor rumble out of the room mic
Lo Cut (In 1)optionalThe M200's own channel low-cut has already shaped the vocals
Sample rate48 kHzPlenty. 96 kHz disables EQ and overdub on this model for no benefit here
Gainroughly rightSet it once via the MIXER button and leave it

32-bit float does not mean levels don't exist — there is a gain control. It means a wrong setting is recoverable in post rather than a ruined take. You still set it approximately once; you just can't destroy a recording by getting it wrong, and nobody has to ride gain during practice.

The one real limit: 32-bit float only removes converter clipping. If the capsule itself overloads, that distortion is baked in and unrecoverable. The built-in X/Y handles 130 dB SPL, and you're a long way from the kit, so this is not a practical concern at this position — but it's why capsule headroom still matters and why moving the recorder much closer to the drums would be a bad idea.

04 · Aiming it

125° is not something you can eyeball — use the landmark

The bearing from the desk to MAIN L is 124.2°, one degree off the specified aim. So "point it at the left main" and the geometry is correct, including the wedge rejection. No protractor needed.

Why that direction and not another: it's the bisector of the instrument spread, and it turns the recorder's back on the two things you most want out of the room track — the wedge 80 units north, and MIC 1's singer, who ends up about 160° off-axis, nearly directly behind the capsules.

05 · What the stereo image will sound like

Wide and hollow-centred, and that's expected

The H4essential's X/Y is fixed at 90° — there's no capsule-angle adjustment on this model. But the instruments span roughly 210° as seen from the desk, because the desk sits inside the band rather than outside looking in. Most sources therefore fall outside the imaging window:

SourceOff-axisWhere it lands
B1 bass amp23°Inside the field, placed naturally
MIC 2 singer24°Inside the field
Drums73°Toward hard left
G2 amp105°Hard left
G1 amp105°Hard right

Outside the window does not mean unrecorded. Both capsules are cardioid and hear nearly everything around them. The 90° figure governs where things sit in the stereo picture, not what gets captured. The drums will be plenty loud — just hard-panned rather than placed.

This isn't a fault to tune away: no aim fits 210° into 90°. Narrowing the spread would mean favouring two sources and losing the others outright. For a bed track sitting under isolated vocals it's fine, and the reverberant field fills the middle in a room this small.

Channel sides: facing MAIN L, the recorder's left picks up the drums and G2, its right picks up G1 and the bass. Stand behind it looking toward the left main and whatever is on your left lands on the left channel. Swap in GarageBand, or rotate the unit, if you want it the other way round.

06 · What bleeds where

You have one strongly isolated vocal, not two

This is the most important honest caveat on the page. Vocals reach the room track from four places — the two singers acoustically, the wedge, and the mains, which carry vocals only. At the specified aim, relative to the drums:

Vocal source in the roomLevel vs. drumsConsequence
MIC 2's singer+2.8 dBLoudest vocal contributor to the room track
MAIN L−4.8 dBOn-axis; the aim points at it
MAIN R−8.2 dBOff to the side
Wedge−8.6 dBClose, but 145° off-axis
MIC 1's singer−29.7 dBNearly behind the mic — well rejected

Why this matters even with no splitters. Your vocal fader can only do as much as the room track allows. Whatever vocal is already baked into the room capture stays there when you pull the vocal track down — so the bleed figures above set the practical limit on vocal control, in any configuration.

The aim does its job where it counts. MIC 1's singer lands about 30 dB below the drums, so the lead vocal is barely in the room track at all and the fader has real authority over it. That's the right thing to protect, since the lead getting buried was the original problem.

MIC 2's singer is the leak. The bass player's voice is the loudest vocal source in the room track — louder than the drums. Their backing vocal will sit at whatever level the room gives it, largely regardless of the vocal fader. Nothing about the recording rig changes that; it's a consequence of where they stand relative to the desk. If it ever becomes a problem the answer is physical, not electrical: move the recorder, or move the mic.

Note also that the aim points at MAIN L, which carries vocals. That's an accepted consequence, not an oversight — every aim points at something, and the alternatives are worse. In a small room where two singers, a wedge and two vocal-carrying mains all share the space, keeping vocals out of a room mic entirely is not achievable. Reducing the worst contributor is.

07 · Getting it into GarageBand

If the laptop is already on the table, record straight into it

This is the lowest-effort route, and it sidesteps the 32-bit float problem entirely — because GarageBand is recording, not importing. There is no post-session file handling at all: practice ends and the session is already in a project.

The H4essential is a 4-in / 2-out USB-C interface, it streams 32-bit float, and critically it keeps recording to the SD card at the same time. So a USB dropout, a GarageBand crash or a mis-armed track costs you nothing — the card has an independent 32-bit float master of the whole session regardless. USB-C also bus-powers the recorder, so no batteries.

  1. USB-C to the MacBook, and set the recorder to audio-interface mode.
  2. Build the project once and save it as a template. Three tracks — one stereo for the X/Y pair, two mono for Inputs 1 and 2. Every later session is: open template, hit record.
  3. Turn on the Record Enable buttons. GarageBand hides these by default, which is the single most common reason multitrack recording "doesn't work" — reveal them via Track → Configure Track Header → Record Enable, then arm all three.
  4. Check the channel mapping once. Which USB channel carries the X/Y pair versus Inputs 1 and 2 is worth confirming on the device rather than assuming; note it down and the template is correct forever.
  5. Stop the laptop sleeping mid-session. Energy Saver, or run caffeinate in Terminal.
  6. Let the card record too. It costs nothing and it's your safety net.

One thing you give up: GarageBand records at 24-bit, so the float headroom doesn't survive into the project. It survives on the card, which is exactly where you'd want it if a session turns out to be worth mixing properly. Set the gain sensibly per §03 and the GarageBand version will be fine on its own.

Or work from the card

  1. Pull the microSD card, or connect USB-C. Files are named by timestamp — MMDDYY_HHMMSS — with the track suffixes from §01.
  2. Want a quick listen? Play TrLR and you're done. No DAW required.
  3. Want to actually mix? Import TrMic, Tr1 and Tr2 as three tracks. They are sample-locked — same recorder, same clock — so they line up with no alignment work.
  4. Start with the room track as the bed and bring the lead vocal up underneath it. That is the whole point of the rig: set the vocal level after practice instead of fighting for it during.
  5. If a track sounds quiet, just raise it. 32-bit float has enormous headroom below and above nominal; there's no noise penalty for pulling a quiet file up the way there would be at 24-bit.

Test this before you rely on it: GarageBand may refuse 32-bit float files. The H4essential records 32-bit float only — there is no 24-bit option in its menu — and GarageBand has a documented history of rejecting 32-bit WAVs with "the file you are trying to import is 32 bit format and cannot be used." Reports conflict on whether current versions still do. Drag one file in and find out; it takes a minute and decides your whole post-session routine.

If it refuses, you have three ways out, in order of least effort:

  • Don't use a DAW at all. Play TrLR in QuickTime or Music. The 32-bit float restriction is GarageBand's, not macOS's — Core Audio plays these fine. For "how did we sound this week", this is the whole workflow.
  • Convert with a one-liner. afconvert ships with macOS, nothing to install:
    afconvert -f WAVE -d LEI24@48000 in.wav out.wav
    Wrap it in a Finder Quick Action and it becomes right-click → convert.
  • Record straight into GarageBand over USB. No files are imported at all, so the format question never arises. If the laptop is already on the table this is the least-effort route, not the most — see the top of this section.

One catch when converting. 32-bit float can hold values above 0 dBFS; 24-bit integer cannot. If a take was recorded hot, conversion will clip exactly what float was protecting. Set the gain roughly right per §03 and this never arises — which is the one reason not to be completely cavalier about levels.

How much fits on a card

32-bit float at 48 kHz costs 691 MB per hour per channel. The recommended setup writes six channels — the X/Y pair, both inputs, and the stereo mix — so about 4.2 GB an hour.

CardRecommended setupPractices it holds
256 GB≈ 62 hoursAbout 21 three-hour sessions
128 GB≈ 31 hoursAbout 10
64 GB≈ 15 hoursAbout 5

256 GB is comfortably more than you need — roughly five months of weekly three-hour practices before it fills. The recorder supports up to 1 TB microSDXC, so the card size is not a constraint; what matters more is speed. Use U3 / V30 or better; slower cards risk dropouts and corrupted files on six simultaneous channels.

Two things to expect. At 96 kHz everything doubles, to about 31 hours on a 256 GB card — another reason to stay at 48. And because WAV files are capped, the recorder auto-splits long recordings: stereo files roughly every 1.4 hours, mono every 2.9. A three-hour practice therefore yields numbered segments rather than one continuous file. That's normal, handled automatically, and one more reason the USB route is pleasant — GarageBand just records a single continuous take.

08 · Check these on the first session

Four things the design assumes but hasn't proven

These are honest open items, not hypotheticals. All four are cheap to test.

  • Wedge bleed. Record a minute with the wedge muted, then a minute unmuted, and compare the room track. If the lead vocal is still prominent in TrMic with Tr1 pulled down, the isolation is compromised and the fix is the B-5 variant in §10.
  • Does GarageBand open the files? The recorder writes 32-bit float only, and GarageBand has historically refused that format. Drag one file in before you build a routine around it — §07 has the fallbacks.
  • Does STEREO OUT follow MASTER LEVEL? Record a few seconds, turn the PA up, record a few more, and compare. If the recording level moved, you'll need to set PA volume before you set record level and leave it alone (§11).
  • MIC 1's three-way split — only if you run a splitter configuration. MIC 1 would then feed the wedge, the M200 and the recorder: three preamps on one capsule. Listen for the lead sounding thin or quiet compared to before. Remedy is a single transformer splitter on that channel. Don't buy one pre-emptively. The recommended setup avoids this entirely by not splitting anything.
  • Room-track balance. If G1 is too buried or the drums dominate too much, the alternative position is in §10.
  • Hum. Passive splitting ties the M200 and the recorder together through cable shields. If you get 60 Hz hum, put both on the same power strip so they share a ground reference — that costs nothing and fixes most ground loops. The recorder running on batteries instead of USB power also breaks the loop entirely.

10 · If this isn't enough

Two upgrades, neither of which wastes what you bought

Better room capture, zero cost — and the cost is lower than it looks. You already own a Behringer B-5. Put its omni capsule on a boom at (500, 350) into Input 1, with 48 V on that channel only, and move MIC 1 to Input 2 with phantom off. The recorder stays on the desk where it's reachable, while an actual microphone occupies the position the acoustics want.

This fixes both weaknesses of the desk position at once. At (500, 350) the instrument spread is 127° rather than 210°, and an omni has no off-axis loss at all — so the hollow-centre problem in §05 and G1's burial in §06 both go away. You end up with two room perspectives to blend: close and drum-forward from the desk, balanced and even from the boom.

The trade is MIC 2's isolated track — which §06 already showed is leaky. The bass player's voice is the loudest vocal source in the room track regardless, so Tr2 was never a clean fader. Giving it up costs materially less than the track count suggests. If you own one B-5 and want one upgrade, this is the one.

Two other targets are possible on that same input and worth knowing, though neither is as strong: the cardioid capsule aimed at G1, a surgical fix for the one badly buried source; or the B-5 as a drum overhead — the kit is the largest uncaptured element, though it's also the closest loud source to the desk and is already well represented in the room track, so this adds less than it first appears.

Electrically this is clean: phantom reaches only the B-5, which is directly connected and not split, while the MIC 1 split junction stays DC-free. That separation is exactly what per-input phantom buys, and it is precisely what the L-8 — with its single global 48 V switch — cannot do.

All four vocals isolated — six tracks. Bring the LiveTrak L-8 you already own. The H4essential's 3.5 mm line out feeds the L-8's channels 7/8, which take TS phone jacks or a TRRS mini, so a 3.5 mm TRS → dual ¼" TS cable (about $10) is the unambiguous connection. Split MIC 3 and MIC 4 as well and feed all four vocals into channels 1–4:

L-8 channelSourceGives you
1–4MIC 1–4 splitsAll four vocals isolated
7/8H4e line outStereo room, sample-locked to the vocals
—H4e microSD32-bit float room master, recorded simultaneously

Phantom stays off, and that matters. All four vocal mics are dynamics, and the room mic arrives at line level on 7/8 rather than on a mic preamp — so nothing on the L-8 needs 48 V and no DC reaches a split junction. The L-8's global phantom switch is only a problem if you put a condenser on one of its mic inputs. It's a constraint on condensers, not on the L-8 as such.

Costs. The L-8 travels every session — which is the constraint that pushed the design away from it originally. Two more splitters. And the room track lands at 24-bit in the L-8, because the handoff is analogue; 32-bit float doesn't survive it. Mitigate by letting the H4essential record its own card at the same time: synced 24-bit for easy mixing, plus an unclippable master when a session is worth aligning by hand.

One gotcha. The H4essential's line out is its headphone jack, so its level follows the volume knob. Set it once, tape it, and don't let anyone nudge it between sessions or your room-track level moves.

Both at once — B-5 through the handheld, into the L-8. These two upgrades combine, and the combination is better than either alone:

B-5 omni, boom @ (500,350) ──► H4e INPUT 1  [48 V on Input 1 only]
H4e built-in X/Y, on the desk ──► H4e MIC
                              ├─ line out (stereo mix) ──► L-8 ch 7/8
                              └─ microSD: TrMic + Tr1, raw 32-bit float
MIC 1-4 ──► splitters ──┬──► M200 ──► MAIN L/R
                          └──► L-8 ch 1-4

Yes, the B-5 is included in the line out. That jack carries the stereo mix of every enabled input, and the manual confirms its level is affected by the Mixer Screen. So whatever you set on the H4e's mixer is what the L-8 receives on 7/8 — one pre-blended room track.

Set Rec Source to Pre Mixer (Rec Settings menu) and the blend is committed only for the L-8. The card still writes TrMic and Tr1 as raw, independent 32-bit float files. Get the blend wrong and nothing is lost — remix from the card. On Post Mixer the mixer settings bake into the individual files too, which is not what you want here.

Why route the B-5 through the handheld rather than straight into the L-8? This is the structural reason, not just convenience. The B-5 needs 48 V; the L-8's phantom is global. Plug the B-5 into an L-8 mic input and you put DC on all four vocal split junctions. Run it through the H4essential and the 48 V stays inside the handheld, on Input 1 only — the L-8 never sees it. The handheld acts as a phantom-isolating preamp. It is the correct way to use a condenser alongside split dynamics on that mixer, and it leaves L-8 channels 5/6 free.

Pan the B-5 centre. The desk X/Y produces the wide, hollow-centred image described in §05 — sources bunched at the extremes. A mono omni panned up the middle fills exactly that hole. The two capture methods are complementary rather than redundant, which is the real argument for running both.

11 · The option space

Ranked by effort, because that's what decides whether you use it

The goal here is the best sound for the least effort — this is a tool for hearing whether the band is getting tighter, not a studio session. Everything below is ranked by effort, not by capability, because the highest-capability rig is the one you eventually stop bothering to set up.

Recommended

Recorder on the desk. B-5 on a boom at (500, 350) into Input 2. M200 STEREO OUT into Input 1. Two cables, no splitters.

Then remove the friction permanently: leave the boom stand and both cables in the room. A mic stand is furniture, not recording gear. Coil the two cables at the desk. You carry only the recorder and the mic.

Session setup: clip the B-5 on, plug two cables, phantom on Input 1, press record. Under a minute. And set the mixer blend once so every session writes a TrLR you can just play — most weeks you will never open GarageBand at all.

All the configurations use the H4essential and differ only in what feeds its two XLR inputs. Input 1 should always be the M200 feed — it's one cable and it's what solves the original quiet-vocals problem. The only real decision is Input 2.

Input 2 is B-5 or MIC 1, not both. For this purpose the B-5 wins: once the M200 feed has given you a vocal fader, isolating the lead on top of that is a refinement, whereas room quality is what decides whether the recording is pleasant to listen back to at all.

The splitter-based configurations are the ones to skip. Cable surgery on two to four microphones, every session, is the highest-friction step in any of these and the one most likely to make you stop bothering. What it buys — per-singer faders — is not what you need to hear whether the band is getting tighter. The full four-splitter L-8 rig in §10 remains documented for the day you want to actually mix something, not for weekly use.

The M200 feed is STEREO OUT, and only STEREO OUT. The mixer has no line-level monitor jack — MONITOR LEVEL sets the volume of Speaker 2, an amplified output. Since channels 1–4 have no pan controls, mono sources appear equally in both halves of STEREO OUT, so the left channel alone carries the whole vocal mix. A 3.5 mm → ¼" TS cable into one input is all it takes; no summing adapter.

C is the recommended one above — zero splitters, nothing at the mics changes, MIC 1 stays a two-way split exactly as it is today, and the three-way loading question in §08 never arises.

If you do want the lead isolated, B beats A. One splitter instead of two, and it delivers more: the lead on its own fader plus every vocal as a controllable group, including MIC 3 and MIC 4, which in A exist only as room bleed. Since §06 showed MIC 2's isolated track is leaky anyway, A's second splitter buys very little. The lead lands on both tracks, which is harmless — the M200 is fully analogue, so there's no delay to cause comb filtering.

Watch out for two things on any M200-fed configuration. The mixer's ECHO knobs are per-channel and analogue — whatever echo is dialled in gets baked into your recording permanently. Check they're down before you record anything you care about. And it is unverified whether STEREO OUT's level follows the MASTER LEVEL knob; if it does, changing PA volume mid-session changes your recording level. Test it once and note the answer.

12 · The session plan

Record continuously, break once at 90 minutes, split later

Yes — one continuous take, not song-by-song. Starting and stopping per song means someone has to remember, every time, and the take you most want is always the unplanned one that began before anybody pressed record. Continuous recording costs one button press for the whole practice and never misses anything.

With one exception, and it's a hard technical limit. GarageBand writes recordings as fixed-point audio in a container capped at 2 GB, which for the stereo X/Y track works out to about 1.9 hours:

TrackCeiling at 24-bitNotes
Stereo (X/Y pair)≈ 1.9 hThe binding constraint
Mono (Input 1 / 2)≈ 3.9 hNot the problem

So stop and restart GarageBand once at a natural break around 90 minutes — between songs, while someone's retuning. That leaves 26 minutes of margin and costs you one interaction per practice. The SD card keeps rolling straight through the gap, so even the moment between stopping and restarting is captured. Nothing can actually be lost.

Once, before the first session

  1. Format the card in the recorder. Set 48 kHz.
  2. Phantom ON for Input 2 (B-5), OFF for Input 1 (M200 line feed). Lo Cut on Input 2.
  3. Set gain roughly during a loud song, then tape the volume knob — it doubles as the line-out level.
  4. Confirm the USB channel mapping and write it down.
  5. Build the GarageBand project: one stereo track for the X/Y, two mono for the inputs. Reveal Record Enable (Track → Configure Track Header), arm all three, save as a template.
  6. Test whether GarageBand opens a 32-bit float file from the card. The answer decides your fallback (§07).
  7. Leave the boom stand and both cables in the room permanently.

Every session — about a minute

  1. Recorder on the desk, aimed at MAIN L. Sitting on foam, not bare desk.
  2. Clip the B-5 on the boom → Input 2. M200 cable → Input 1.
  3. USB-C to the laptop. Run caffeinate so it can't sleep.
  4. Open the template, hit record. Start the card recording too.
  5. Play. Ignore the equipment.
  6. Around 90 minutes, at a natural pause: stop and restart the GarageBand recording.
  7. At the end, stop and save as a dated project.

Two habits that make a 3-hour file navigable

Press the recorder's MARK button when something is worth finding again. It drops a mark in the file mid-recording, and FF/REW jump between marks on playback. One button, no interruption, no note-taking.

Say the song name before you play it. "Okay — Wagon Wheel, take three." It costs nothing, it's captured on the recording, and scrubbing a long file becomes navigable by ear. This is what studios call slating, and it exists precisely because it solves this problem better than any amount of software.

Afterwards

Just listening? Play TrLR from the card — QuickTime or Music, no DAW. AirDrop it to your phone and listen in the car. For "how did we sound this week", this is the whole workflow.

Working on something? Open the project. Song boundaries show up as visible gaps in the waveform — put the playhead in the gap and press ⌘T to split, then delete what you don't want. Do this only for songs you actually care about; there is no reason to tidy the whole session.

Don't organise the recordings you'll never open. The point is to hear whether the band is getting tighter. Most sessions get played once and that's the correct amount of effort to spend on them.

Why this design and not something simpler

The obvious approach — one room mic, or a line feed from the mixer — cannot work here. Placement can't fix it because the mains and the drums are both at the south end, so every step toward the vocals is a step toward the kit; the best mains-to-drums ratio available anywhere in this room is −1.7 dB. And the M200 can't help because it exposes only STEREO OUT (everything summed) and MONITOR OUT (a mono mix of everything) — no direct outs, no channel inserts. Nothing leaving that mixer contains an isolated vocal. Splitting at the microphone is the only route to per-singer control. The full reasoning, with sources, is Principle 07 on the references page.
